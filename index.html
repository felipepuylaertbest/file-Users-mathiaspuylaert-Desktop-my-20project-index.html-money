Start with your diploma

What can you study?

Your Liceo Scientifico maturità is a strong starting point for undergraduate study. The Danish institution checks your diploma against its entry rules, then looks closely at your subjects, grades and language proof.

01 / UNIVERSITY

Bachelor’s degree

Usually 3 years. Choose a subject like engineering, computer science, natural sciences, business or social sciences. Maths and science subject levels can matter for specific courses.

02 / UNIVERSITY COLLEGE

Professional bachelor

Typically 3–4 years, often mixing academic study with practical projects or placements. Fields include design, engineering, business and health-related professions.

03 / ACADEMY

Academy profession

A shorter, career-focused route, often around 2 years. Some programmes accept a general upper-secondary diploma; others ask for relevant vocational preparation.

The subject check

“Scientific high school” doesn’t automatically guarantee every prerequisite. A course may ask for a particular level of maths, physics or another subject. Send your transcript to the admissions office and ask them to confirm equivalency before you apply. If you’re missing a subject, supplementary courses may be possible, but they are offered in Danish.

Your future has more than one direction

Explore the whole map.

The scientific high school note helps explain your current subjects; it does not define what you can be interested in. Denmark has paths across science, business, society, care, creativity and hands-on work. No job is guaranteed to be AI-proof, so think about the daily work you would enjoy as well as where your strengths might grow.

01 / BUILD THE NEXT MACHINERobotics + automation

Robotics, mechatronics & control

Design robots, sensors and automated systems; make them work safely in factories, hospitals or transport. Great if you like physics, coding and making things move.

Could lead to: robotics engineer, automation engineer, controls specialist. Physical integration and safety work can be harder to automate end-to-end.
02 / POWER THE TRANSITIONClimate + energy

Renewable energy & electrical engineering

Work on wind power, grids, batteries and energy storage. Expect a mix of maths, physics, simulation and practical infrastructure challenges.

Could lead to: renewable-energy engineer, grid analyst, storage systems engineer. Demand is linked to long-term infrastructure and decarbonisation work.
03 / SCIENCE THAT HELPS PEOPLEHealth + biotech

Biomedical engineering & biotechnology

Connect biology with engineering: medical devices, diagnostics, lab automation or bioprocessing. A lively fit for students who like both biology and maths.

Could lead to: biomedical engineer, biotech process specialist, clinical technology or quality roles. Regulation, lab practice and patient safety add important human responsibility.
04 / DEFEND THE DIGITAL WORLDSecurity + computing

Cybersecurity & secure software

Learn networks, secure systems, cryptography and incident response. AI changes the tools on both sides, so the durable edge is deep systems knowledge and sound judgment.

Could lead to: security engineer, incident responder, security architect. Threats and systems keep changing, so continuous learning is part of the job.
05 / DESIGN BETTER PLACESInfrastructure + climate

Civil engineering & climate adaptation

Plan resilient buildings, transport, water and cities for a changing climate. Combines design software with site realities, regulations and public needs.

Could lead to: civil engineer, water-resilience planner, sustainable-building consultant. Construction and infrastructure face ongoing European skills needs.
06 / MAKE TECH WORK FOR HUMANSHealth + human factors

Health technology & human-centred design

Shape medical software and devices around clinicians and patients. Blend programming, design, biology and research to solve problems where usability matters.

Could lead to: health-tech designer, usability researcher, medical-device product specialist. Human needs, accessibility and safety matter as much as the code.
07 / MAKE IDEAS WORKBusiness + finance

Business, economics & entrepreneurship

Explore how companies, markets and money work; specialise in marketing, finance, accounting, people or starting a venture.

Could lead to: business analyst, accountant (with required qualifications), marketing or HR specialist, consultant, founder. AI will change routine analysis, while judgment, relationships and domain knowledge remain valuable.
08 / UNDERSTAND PEOPLE & POWERSociety + policy

Politics, international relations & sociology

Study institutions, communities, migration, conflict and public policy. Often a reading-, writing- and research-heavy route.

Could lead to: policy or research assistant, NGO programme officer, public-sector analyst, advocacy or international project roles. Strong languages, data skills and internships help turn broad study into a focused career.
09 / LISTEN, SUPPORT, GUIDEPsychology + wellbeing

Psychology & behavioural science

Learn how people think, feel and act, using experiments and evidence. Becoming a licensed psychologist typically requires further study and country-specific approval.

Could lead to: research assistant, user researcher, people and culture roles, wellbeing programme work; with further qualifications, clinical or counselling pathways.
10 / TEACH THE NEXT GENERATIONEducation

Teaching & education

Learn to design lessons, explain ideas and support different learners. Danish school teaching normally requires Danish-language study and local professional requirements.

Could lead to: teacher, learning designer, education coordinator, tutor or training specialist. Human connection, classroom leadership and adapting to individual students are central.
11 / CARE IN REAL LIFEHealth professions

Nursing, physiotherapy & occupational therapy

Practical, people-facing study for anyone drawn to health, movement and helping people regain independence. Many qualifying programmes and placements are in Danish.

Could lead to: nurse, physiotherapist, occupational therapist or healthcare coordinator, subject to local registration and language requirements. Care roles are growing, but the work is demanding and very human.
12 / TELL STORIES & MAKE CULTUREArts + media

Film, music, visual arts & communication

Make work for stage, screen, games, galleries or audiences; courses may ask for a portfolio, audition or creative entrance test.

Could lead to: performer, musician, producer, editor, journalist, communications specialist, arts administrator or freelance creator. Build a portfolio and practical experience alongside the degree.
13 / DESIGN HOW WE LIVEArchitecture + design

Architecture, product & fashion design

Shape buildings, objects, clothing or services through sketching, models, materials and digital tools. Creative admissions can be portfolio-based.

Could lead to: architect (with required professional pathway), product or service designer, urban design assistant, fashion designer, model maker. AI can speed up concepts; material, client and site decisions still matter.
14 / FEED & CARE FOR THE PLANETFood + environment

Agriculture, food science & animal care

Study crops, animals, food production, ecosystems or veterinary science. Veterinary entry is highly competitive and usually needs strong science prerequisites.

Could lead to: agronomist, food quality specialist, sustainability advisor, animal health professional or veterinarian after the required professional degree.
15 / MOVE PEOPLE & GOODSTravel + logistics

Tourism, hospitality & supply chains

Plan memorable visitor experiences, run hotels and events, or keep goods moving around the world. These routes can be very practical and placement-rich.

Could lead to: hotel or event manager, destination coordinator, logistics planner, procurement specialist or shipping operations roles. Communication, operations and language skills make a difference.
16 / WORK WITH THE SEAMaritime + transport

Shipping, marine engineering & navigation

Explore ports, ships, marine systems and global trade. Some professional routes require sea-time, medical checks, certifications or Danish instruction.

Could lead to: marine engineer, deck officer after certification, port operations planner, maritime logistics specialist or shipping analyst.
17 / MOVE, COACH, PERFORMSport + movement

Sport science & coaching

Explore exercise, performance, health and how people learn movement. Combine science with coaching practice and communication.

Could lead to: sports development officer, coach (with relevant qualifications), exercise specialist, performance analyst or community health coordinator.
18 / ASK BIG QUESTIONSNatural sciences

Physics, chemistry, maths & biology

Choose a deep science degree to investigate how the world works, from particles and climate to cells, materials and statistics.

Could lead to: laboratory or research roles, data analyst, science communicator, quality specialist or further study toward research and teaching.
19 / SHAPE THE RULESLaw + governance

Law, public administration & governance

Study rights, institutions, regulation and how societies make decisions. Danish legal practice requires Danish-language qualifications; international policy and compliance are other routes.

Could lead to: lawyer after the required national degree and authorisation, legal assistant, compliance analyst, public administration or policy roles.
20 / CONNECT ACROSS LANGUAGESLanguages + humanities

History, languages & cultural studies

Explore how people communicate, remember and create meaning. Pair the subject with digital research, teaching, publishing, archives or a regional specialism.

Could lead to: editor, translator (with strong professional language skills), researcher, archivist, cultural project coordinator or communications roles. Build experience and a clear specialism.
How to use this list

This is a map of subject areas, not a guarantee that every named degree is available as an English-taught bachelor in Denmark. The official catalogue mixes bachelor, master, exchange and short programmes, and is being revised; use its filters and confirm the exact 2027 intake with the school. Some fields—especially healthcare, teaching and regulated professions—often need Danish and additional qualifications. The scientific maturità gives you a starting point, but each course checks its own subject prerequisites. Labour forecasts describe trends, not an individual job guarantee.

Admissions, without the mystery

How to get in

Most undergraduate applications are coordinated through Optagelse.dk, then assessed by each institution. Start early: international documents and course-by-course requirements take time to check.

Pick a programme

Search for a course and confirm it accepts your diploma. Check required subjects and whether it is taught in English or Danish.

Check language

English-taught courses require strong English, often around Danish English B. Some accept school records; others require IELTS, TOEFL or Cambridge.

Apply by 15 March

Non-Danish qualifications and quota 2 applications generally have a noon CET deadline on 15 March via Optagelse.dk. You can rank up to 8 programmes.

Upload documents

Prepare your maturità, transcript and any requested translations or language proof. Follow the institution’s instructions for files and deadlines.

Will there be an entrance exam?

Often there is no single national entrance exam. Competitive places may be allocated by grades (quota 1), programme-specific criteria (quota 2), or a test/interview for certain courses. Check the exact admissions page for every programme you list.

Good questions, straight answers

Before you pack.

Rules can vary by programme and citizenship. Use these answers as a starting point, then confirm details with the school.

Does Denmark help with tuition or living costs?

EU/EEA students generally do not pay tuition at eligible Danish higher education programmes. The state grant (SU) is separate: EU citizens may qualify under specific rules, commonly by working in Denmark around 10–12 hours weekly on an ongoing basis, or through another qualifying status. It is not automatic. There is no general transport subsidy simply for being an international student, though eligible students may buy a discounted Ungdomskort pass.

Can I use my maturità?

Yes, it can be assessed as an international upper-secondary qualification. The institution decides if it meets the general entry level and your specific course prerequisites. Provide your diploma and subject grades, and ask admissions to assess them before the deadline.

Do I need an entrance exam?

Usually not a universal exam. Some courses are selective and may use quota 2 criteria, interviews, auditions or a programme-specific test. Meeting minimum requirements does not guarantee a place where applications exceed available seats.

Can I study in English?

Yes. Denmark has many English-taught options, but availability differs by subject and level. English proof may be required; the institution sets accepted tests and score thresholds. Danish-taught programmes require Danish proficiency.

What should I budget for housing?

Official estimates put rent around DKK 3,000–6,500 per month, with large differences by city and housing type. Student halls are an option, and the official guide recommends looking early, especially before August and September.

What if I’m not an EU citizen?

Tuition and residence rules depend on citizenship and residence status. Many non-EU students pay tuition and need a study residence permit. Check the current official rules for your specific situation before making plans.
